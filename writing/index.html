31 March 2022

Volcanoes, diamonds, and blobs: a billion-year history of Earth's interior shows it's more mobile than we thought

With Nicolas Flament, Andrew Merdith and Ömer F. Bodur. Computer simulations spanning a billion years show that two continent-sized blobs of hot rock deep in Earth's mantle (one under Africa, one under the Pacific) have assembled and broken apart more than previously thought, in a rhythm not unlike the way continents gather into supercontinents.

What are lost continents, and why are we discovering so many?

With Maria Seton and Joanne Whittaker. Geoscientists identify lost continents by rock composition, not elevation. Many are plateaus of continental crust hidden below sea level. Better ocean mapping and deep-sea sampling are why so many are turning up now.

How we traced the underwater volcanic ancestry of Lord Howe Island

With Maria Seton and Nicholas Mortimer. Rock samples dredged from Horsehead Seamount during a 2012 research voyage reveal that Lord Howe Island belongs to a 1,500km chain of volcanoes built as the Australian plate drifted north over a stationary mantle hotspot.

What is it really like under the Indian Ocean?

With Joanne Whittaker. Less than 10% of the world's seafloor has been mapped in real detail. Ship-based surveys of features like the Batavia Knoll, in the Indian Ocean, do far more than satellites can to reveal how continents broke apart and drifted over millions of years.
